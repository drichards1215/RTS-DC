IThe Statue

Most presidential memorials in Washington stand in full view of traffic and tour buses. Theodore Roosevelt's is hidden on purpose. To find it, you cross a footbridge from the Virginia shore onto a wooded island in the Potomac, then follow a shaded path until the trees open onto a stone plaza. There, a 17-foot bronze Roosevelt stands with his right arm raised, caught in the middle of a speech.

The figure is the work of sculptor Paul Manship, best known for the gilded Prometheus at Rockefeller Center. Architect Eric Gugler designed the plaza around it, flanked by four granite tablets carved with Roosevelt's words on Nature, Manhood, Youth, and The State. The memorial was dedicated in 1967, but the island itself had been set aside in his honor decades earlier and replanted as a native forest, a living monument to the man who made conservation a national cause.

A president remembered not by a marble temple on a busy avenue, but by 88 acres of woods, marsh, and birdsong.

IIThe Man

Theodore Roosevelt was born in New York City in 1858, a sickly, asthmatic child who built himself into a rancher, author, naturalist, and soldier. He led the "Rough Riders" volunteer cavalry in Cuba during the Spanish-American War in 1898 and came home a national hero.

In 1901, after the assassination of President William McKinley, Roosevelt became the 26th president at age 42, still the youngest person ever to hold the office. He took on powerful business monopolies, pushed for safer food and medicine, started work on the Panama Canal, and in 1906 became the first American to win the Nobel Peace Prize, for helping to end the Russo-Japanese War.

His most lasting legacy is the land. Roosevelt protected roughly 230 million acres of American wilderness, creating national forests, bird reserves, national parks, and national monuments. And yes, the teddy bear is named after him: it was inspired by a 1902 hunting trip on which he refused to shoot a captured bear.

He died in 1919 at his home in Oyster Bay, New York.

IIIVisiting

The island is reached by a footbridge from a parking area off the George Washington Memorial Parkway on the Virginia side of the river. It's an easy walk from the bridge to the memorial plaza, and trails loop around the rest of the island. There's no car access on the island itself.
