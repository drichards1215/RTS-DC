Marquis de Lafayette

Lafayette Square

Sculptor

1891
Material
Bronze, marble
Setting

IThe Statue

Lafayette Square is named for him, and in its southeast corner, facing the White House, the Marquis de Lafayette stands high on a marble pedestal, his hand outstretched. The pose shows him appealing to the French National Assembly to support the American Revolution.

The monument was made in France by sculptors Alexandre Falguière and Antonin Mercié and dedicated in 1891. Around its base, bronze figures of French officers who fought for American independence keep him company, and a woman representing America reaches up to offer him a sword.

Lafayette's statue was the first of four in the square's corners honoring foreign officers who helped win the Revolution. The others are the Comte de Rochambeau, Baron von Steuben, and Tadeusz Kościuszko.

A Frenchman at the White House's doorstep, in the square that took his name after his triumphant return in 1824.

IIThe Man

Gilbert du Motier, the Marquis de Lafayette, was born in 1757 into a wealthy noble family in France. In 1777, at just 19, he defied his king and sailed to America at his own expense to join the fight for independence.

Congress made him a major general. He was wounded at the Battle of Brandywine, endured the winter at Valley Forge, and became almost a son to George Washington. In 1781 he helped trap the British army at Yorktown, ending the war's last major campaign.

Back in France, Lafayette helped write the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen in 1789, then spent five years as a political prisoner in Austria. In 1824 and 1825 he returned for a farewell tour of all 24 states, greeted by enormous crowds. When he died in 1834, he was buried in Paris under soil he had brought home from Bunker Hill.

IIIVisiting

The statue is at the southeast corner of Lafayette Square, at Pennsylvania Avenue and Madison Place NW, across from the White House. The nearest Metro station is McPherson Square.
